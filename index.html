The 2060 Problem

In this simulation, you get to live out every policy wonk's dream. You get a population, in this case one shaped like the United States in 2025, and the power to make whatever changes you think it needs to look after its aging members. You can change how many children people have, how long they live, and how many people move in. You can also change the rules: when pensions start, how many older people keep working, and how generous the pension is. The only catch is that the payroll tax needed to fund pensions in 2060 has to stay at 15 percent or less. Record your choices on the accompanying worksheet.

Your task
Keep the payroll tax needed to fund pensions at or below 15% in 2060.

What the model assumes (and leaves out)
  • The starting population is rounded and U.S.-shaped, about 337 million people in 2025. Treat it as a teaching population, not an official estimate.
  • Fertility and life expectancy move in a straight line from today's values to your settings by 2050, then stay put. Policy changes take effect immediately, with no phase-in.
  • Mortality comes from a single age pattern scaled up or down to hit your life expectancy. Men and women share one schedule.
  • Births come from women aged 15 to 49 (49% of that age group), using a fixed age pattern of childbearing. Migrants arrive with a fixed age profile concentrated at ages 20 to 34 and then age, have children, and die like everyone else.
  • Workers are people 15 and older times a participation rate for their age group, rounded from recent U.S. figures. Raising the eligibility age above 67 keeps people in the workforce at the rate of 60 to 64 year olds; lowering it pulls them out at the rate of 65 to 69 year olds.
  • The pension is stylized: one flat benefit, pay-as-you-go, no trust fund, no disability benefits, no wage growth. Real U.S. Social Security is more complicated, which is not the same as being better funded.
  • Relative health costs by age are rounded, illustrative values. People 85 and older are assumed to cost about four times as much as the average young adult.
  • Nothing feeds back. Wages, productivity, automation, and whether anyone actually wants to have 2.6 children are outside the model.
